Here is what being local actually buys you, what it does not, and the seven questions that tell you inside one conversation whether a lender is real.

What "near me" actually buys you

Less than you would think, and more than you would think, depending on which kind of local you mean.

A hard money loan is a business-purpose loan secured by an investment property. It is not a mortgage on your house, and the money does not care where the office is. Appraisals, title, inspections and closings are handled by professionals in the property's own market, and the wire lands the same whether the lender sits fifteen minutes away or two states over.

What genuinely has to be local is knowledge, and that is a different thing from an address.

A lender who has never closed in Maryland may not know that most rentals built before 1978 must be registered with the state and pass a lead risk reduction inspection at each change in occupancy, which decides when the house can actually be rented. A lender who has never closed in Texas may not warn you that the seller's homestead exemption and its 10 percent cap on appraisal increases end with the sale, so the tax bill you inherit on paper is not the one you will pay. In Florida it is the insurance cost. In Cleveland it is the city's rental registration and the lead-safe certificate an older rental needs.

None of that requires an office on your street. It requires someone who has actually done deals where you are buying. When you call anyone, us included, ask how many deals they have closed in your state in the last year. The answer to that question is worth more than the pin on the map.

The seven questions, and what a real answer sounds like

You can settle most of this in a single phone call. Ask these in order and pay attention less to the numbers than to how specific the answers are. Vagueness on any one of them is the warning.

1. What is the interest rate on my deal, and is it fixed for the full term? A real lender quotes a rate or a tight range against your scenario. "It depends" with nothing following it is not an answer.

2. How many points, and when are they due? They should be able to say the number immediately. Points belong in your profit math, not a footnote.

3. What is the maximum loan to value and loan to cost, and which one will bind on my deal? Both ceilings get calculated and the smaller wins. A lender who only quotes the flattering one is selling, not underwriting.

4. Is interest charged on the full balance or only on funds drawn? On a rehab with a draw schedule this is thousands of dollars. Get the answer before the term sheet.

5. How are draws released, and how long does an inspection take? Rehab money is commonly reimbursed after work is inspected, not advanced before it starts. If they cannot describe the process, they have not done many.

6. What happens if the project runs past the maturity date, and what does an extension cost? Extension pricing should be a stated number in writing, not a shrug.

7. Who actually makes the lending decision, and whose money is it? A direct lender and a firm that arranges financing through lending partners are both legitimate structures. Someone who is cagey about which one they are is not.

Write the answers down. If the numbers move between that call and the loan documents, you are looking at a bait and switch, and the shift itself is the signal, not the size of it.

The fee pattern that should end the conversation

Advance-fee schemes are common enough in lending that the pattern deserves naming directly. The Federal Trade Commission describes it the same way every time: a promise of a loan whatever your situation, a request for money up front to "secure" it or cover "processing," and then delay, another fee, or nothing at all.

Some upfront costs are normal. An appraisal, a credit report, sometimes an application fee: those are real services with real invoices behind them.

What is not normal is being asked for a substantial sum before anyone has looked at your deal, your property or your financials. The test is simple: ask in writing exactly what service the fee buys. A real answer exists for an appraisal. There is no real answer for a "commitment fee" on a loan nobody has underwritten.

The other flags worth knowing:

Approval promised before anyone has seen the deal. Hard money underwrites the asset rather than your tax returns, but it still underwrites. A lender who does not want to see the deal is not planning to fund it.

No written term sheet you can take away. A reputable lender puts terms in writing and lets you think about it. Pressure to accept on the spot usually exists so you cannot research them.

Nothing down, on your first deal. Offers built to remove every obstacle between a new investor and a first purchase are aimed precisely at people who want to hear it. That is when to slow down and ask more questions, not fewer.

Wire requests to a person, or gift cards and money orders. You have almost no recourse once a wire is sent. Real loan funds and fees move through a title company or closing attorney.

Before you send anyone anything: confirm the company has a real physical location, check its record with the Better Business Bureau, and look it up with your state attorney general's consumer protection office. Ten minutes, and it is the cheapest diligence in this business.

Questions about finding a local lender

Does my hard money lender have to be in my state?

No. The loan is business purpose and secured by the property, and the appraisal, title work and closing happen locally wherever the lender sits. What matters is that the lender works in your state and has closed deals in your market before.

Is a local bank better than a hard money lender?

For a finished property with a long timeline, often yes: bank money is cheaper. For a distressed purchase, an auction, or anything that will not appraise until after the rehab, a bank generally will not lend at all, and that is the gap hard money exists to fill.

Should I pay a fee before a lender reviews my deal?

Not a large one. An appraisal or credit report has an invoice behind it. A sizable fee to "secure" or "lock in" a loan before you have written terms is the pattern the Federal Trade Commission warns about in advance-fee scams.

What should I ask a hard money lender on the first call?

The rate and whether it is fixed, the points, the maximum loan to value and loan to cost, whether interest runs on drawn funds only, how draws work, what an extension costs, and who makes the lending decision. Write the answers down and compare them to the term sheet.

How do I check that a hard money lender near me is legitimate?

Confirm a real physical address, check the company with the Better Business Bureau and your state attorney general, insist on written terms, and make sure money moves through a title company or closing attorney rather than a wire to a person.
