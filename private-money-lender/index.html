Private Money Lenders Near Me: Where Local Private Money Really Comes From

"Private money lender" covers two very different things.

One is a person, a retired landlord or a dentist with savings, lending their own money against your deal. The other is a private lending company with published programs and its own underwriting. Both can be near you, and both can be the right choice. This page shows where to find each one, how a loan from a person should be put on paper to protect you both, and the signs that a "private lender" is neither.

Private money is not a bank

A private money lender is a non-bank source of financing for real estate investment deals. The capital comes from either an individual lending their own savings or a lending company that pools investor funds. Either way, the underwriting is fundamentally different from a conventional mortgage: the lender evaluates the property and the deal, not the borrower's W-2 income and two years of tax returns.

That difference is why private money exists. A fix-and-flip investor buying a distressed property at auction cannot wait 45 days for a bank to process an application. A rental investor whose income comes from twelve LLCs cannot fit it onto a Fannie Mae 1003. A property that needs a new roof, updated electrical, and a kitchen renovation does not meet the habitability standards a conventional lender requires before funding. Private money solves all three problems, speed, flexibility, and the willingness to lend on what the property will be, not just what it is today.

How it works

A private money loan is a short-term, interest-only loan secured by the investment property. The borrower pays interest monthly and repays the principal at maturity, typically by selling the property (fix and flip) or refinancing into a long-term rental loan (fix and hold). The entire process, from application to funded, usually takes 7 to 21 days.

The lender's underwriting focuses on three things: the property's current value (as-is), the value after repair (ARV), and the borrower's exit strategy. If the numbers work, the purchase price plus rehab cost leaves enough room to sell at a profit or refinance at a sustainable payment, the deal gets funded. The borrower's credit score matters, but it is not the gatekeeper it is at a bank. Most private lenders require a 600 to 650 minimum FICO, not the 720-plus that gets you the best conventional rate.

The borrower must be an entity, an LLC, corporation, or trust, and the property must be non-owner-occupied. These are business-purpose loans, not consumer mortgages, which is why they fall outside the disclosure and timing requirements of Regulation Z and TRID.

What it costs

Private money is more expensive than a conventional mortgage. Interest rates typically run 10 to 14 percent, and the lender charges origination points, usually 2 to 4 points, where each point is one percent of the loan amount. On a $200,000 loan at 12 percent and 3 points, the upfront cost is $6,000 in points and the monthly interest payment is $2,000.

The cost is the trade. You are paying for speed, for approval on a property a bank would decline, and for leverage that can include the rehab budget. If the deal's numbers work, and the exit produces a profit or a sustainable rental after refinancing, the higher cost of the short-term loan is a tool cost, not a loss. If the deal's numbers do not work with private money's pricing baked in, the deal does not work, and no amount of cheaper financing changes that.

Rates improve with experience. A borrower who has completed multiple deals and repaid previous loans on time will earn better pricing than a first-timer on the same property. The 10.99 percent starting rate you see advertised by some lenders is loyalty pricing: available after a track record with that lender. A first-time borrower should expect to start above it and earn the way down.

Two kinds of private lender

An individual. Someone with capital in a savings account, a self-directed IRA, or liquid investments, who lends it directly to a real estate investor. The terms are negotiated deal by deal. The advantage is flexibility: an individual lender can structure a deal however they want. The risk is that neither side may know how to paper it correctly, and a handshake deal with no title insurance, no recorded deed of trust, and no draw schedule is a lawsuit waiting to happen.

A lending company. A business that pools capital from multiple investors and lends it through a standardized underwriting process. The terms are set by the company's guidelines, not negotiated from scratch. The advantage is predictability: you know the rate, the points, the draw process, and the timeline before you apply. The disadvantage is less flexibility on edge cases.

When to use private money

  • Speed. The seller wants to close in two weeks. A bank needs six. Private money closes in 7 to 21 days, sometimes faster on clean deals.
  • Property condition. The house needs a roof, a kitchen, and updated plumbing. No conventional lender will fund it in this condition. A private lender will, because the loan includes the rehab budget and the underwriting is based on what the property will be worth after the work.
  • Income documentation. You are self-employed, your income flows through multiple entities, or your last two tax returns do not reflect your actual earnings. Private lenders do not require conventional income documentation for most loan products.
  • Portfolio growth. You already have ten financed properties and Fannie Mae will not approve number eleven. Private money, especially DSCR rental loans, underwrites the property's rental income, not your personal debt-to-income ratio.
  • Auction and short-sale purchases. Cash or cash-equivalent is required to win. Private money can fund in days, which makes it functionally equivalent to cash in the seller's eyes.

When NOT to use it

If you qualify for a conventional loan at 7 percent and the timeline allows it, take it. Private money at 12 percent costs more, and the only reason to pay the premium is when speed, property condition, or documentation prevents the conventional route. Private money is a tool for situations where cheaper financing is not available or not fast enough, not a substitute for it when it is.

If you plan to live in the property, private money is not an option. These loans are for non-owner-occupied investment property only. If you need financing for a home you will occupy, talk to a mortgage broker.

Red flags in a private lender

  • Guaranteed approval. No legitimate lender guarantees approval before seeing the deal. If someone promises funding before they know the property, the address, or the numbers, they are collecting an upfront fee and may never fund anything.
  • Large upfront fees before commitment. Application fees of $100 to $500 for an appraisal or processing are normal. A demand for thousands of dollars before any underwriting begins is not.
  • No written commitment letter. A real lender issues a term sheet or letter of intent that spells out the rate, points, term, fees, and conditions. If the terms only exist in a phone conversation, you have nothing enforceable.
  • Refusal to name the capital source. You should know whether you are borrowing from the person in front of you, from a fund they manage, or from a third-party lender they broker to. The answer changes your legal relationship and your recourse if something goes wrong.
  • Pressure to skip title insurance or an appraisal. Both protect you. A lender who discourages them is either cutting corners or does not intend to fund.

Frequently asked questions

What is a private money lender?

A non-bank source of financing for real estate investment deals. The capital comes from an individual or a lending company, and the underwriting focuses on the property and the deal rather than the borrower's income documentation.

How fast can a private money lender close?

Most close in 7 to 21 days. Some close in under a week on clean deals. The speed comes from simpler underwriting: the lender evaluates the property and the exit strategy, not the borrower's full income history.

What does private money cost?

Interest rates typically run 10 to 14 percent with 2 to 4 origination points. Rates improve with experience: a borrower with completed deals pays less than a first-timer on the same property.

Is private money lending legal?

Yes. Private money lending is legal in all 50 states for business-purpose, non-owner-occupied real estate. It is regulated as a commercial transaction, not consumer credit.

When should I use a private lender instead of a bank?

When speed matters, when the property needs work a bank will not fund, when your income does not fit a conventional box, or when you have maxed out Fannie Mae's financed-property limit.

Do private money lenders check credit?

Most do. The score is not the deciding factor: the property, deal structure, and exit strategy carry more weight. Most require a minimum FICO of 600 to 650.

What is the difference between a private money lender and a hard money lender?

In practice, the terms overlap. Hard money traditionally meant asset-based lending; private money meant an individual lending their own funds. Today both refer to non-bank real estate financing, and the underwriting, speed, and cost structure are the same.
