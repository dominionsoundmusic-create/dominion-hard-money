Fix and Flip Loans

Fix and flip loans are sized on the house's after-repair value, not your paycheck.

Get your deal reviewedFirst flip? Start here

Here is the 70% rule with the arithmetic done, how rehab draws actually work, and what the money costs from offer to resale.

Why a bank will not fund a flip

A conventional mortgage is written on the assumption that the property is habitable, that you will live in it or rent it, and that you will still own it in thirty years. A flip breaks all three assumptions on day one. Banks rarely finance heavy repairs, and even when one would, the approval takes longer than the seller of a distressed property will wait.

Fix and flip loans exist to fill that gap. They are short-term, interest-only, and underwritten on the property's after-repair value rather than on your income, which is why a first-time flipper with a good deal can be financed the same way as somebody on their twentieth.

The 70% rule, with the arithmetic done

This is the single discipline that separates flippers who stay in business from those who do one deal and stop. The maximum you pay is 70% of the after-repair value, minus what the repairs will cost.

Three deals, screened

ARV $300,000, repairs $50,000Max offer $160,000
ARV $320,000, repairs $60,000Max offer $164,000
ARV $400,000, repairs $50,000Max offer $230,000

Multiply the ARV by 0.70, then subtract the repair budget. That is the number you do not go above.

The thirty percent you are leaving on the table is not profit you are giving away. It covers your financing costs, your holding costs while you own it, agent commission and closing costs on the sale, and the things that go wrong. ATTOM, which tracks flips from recorded deeds, notes that renovation and other costs commonly run 20% to 33% of a property's value. Take that margin out and a deal that looked fine on a spreadsheet loses money in the real world.

Adjust it to the market, not to your optimism. Gross flipping margins swing widely between metros: in ATTOM's second-quarter 2026 report the typical flip earned 81.5% over its purchase price in Pittsburgh and lost 0.3% in San Antonio, before any renovation or financing cost. In slower or thinner markets, a 65% screen is the safer one. What you should not do is stretch the rule because you want a particular deal to work.

How draws work, and why it matters

You do not receive the renovation budget on day one. It is released in stages as work is completed and verified. Most first-time flippers underestimate how much this shapes their cash flow.

  • You complete a phase. Demolition, framing, plumbing, electrical, finishes: whatever the agreed milestones are.
  • You submit a draw request with photographs and invoices showing the work is done and paid for.
  • The lender verifies it, usually by sending an inspector. Inspection fees commonly run about $150 to $250 per draw, and some lenders charge more.
  • Funds release, often within three to five business days of an approved inspection.

The practical consequence: you fund each phase out of your own pocket first and get reimbursed. If you have budgeted to the last dollar, the gap between paying your framer and the draw landing is where projects stall. Build your scope of work in phases that align with the draw schedule, and hold cash to cover at least one phase ahead.

The upside of the same structure is that on many loans you are not paying interest on renovation money you have not yet drawn. Ask whether interest accrues on the full commitment or only on funds disbursed: on a slow project the difference is real money.

What a flip actually costs to finance

Do this arithmetic before you make an offer, not at the closing table. On a $200,000 loan at the lending partner's standard 12.99% plus 2.99 points, the points are about $5,980 at closing and interest is about $2,165 a month. Held six months, that is roughly $18,970, or 9.5% of the loan. Held nine months, roughly $25,470, or 12.7%. Every extra month adds about 1.1%.

  • Interest is paid monthly on an interest-only loan, so it scales directly with how long you hold.
  • Points are paid once at closing, whatever your timeline turns out to be.
  • Draw inspection fees recur on every draw, and there will be several.
  • Extension fees, commonly 0.5% to 2% of the loan per extension, if you run past term. Extensions are at the lender's discretion.
  • Prepayment terms. Short-term flip loans generally should not carry a prepayment penalty, because you want to repay the moment it sells. Some lenders add an exit fee instead. Confirm it in writing rather than assuming.

Speed is the cheapest thing you can buy. ATTOM's second-quarter 2026 report put the typical flip at 161 days from purchase to resale, about five and a half months. Every month beyond your plan is another month of interest, taxes, insurance and utilities.

The mistakes that kill deals

Lenders see the same handful over and over, and every one is avoidable.

  • An ARV built on optimistic comps. A common reason an application is declined or downsized. Use real comparable sales from that neighborhood in the last six months, not the best sale in the zip code.
  • A rehab budget that has never met a contractor. Get line-item bids. Underwriting will test the number, and a budget that collapses under scrutiny costs you the deal.
  • Underestimating carrying costs. Interest, taxes, insurance, utilities and lawn care for every month you own it, including the months it sits on the market.
  • No cash between draws. Covered above, and it stalls more projects than anything else.
  • One exit only. If it does not sell, can you refinance and rent it? Model both before you close: a flip that becomes a rental is a setback, and a flip with no second exit can end in default.

Fix and flip questions

What is the 70% rule in house flipping?

Pay no more than 70% of the after-repair value minus repair costs. On a property with a $300,000 ARV needing $50,000 of work, that is a $160,000 maximum offer. The remaining margin covers financing, holding costs, selling costs and the things that go wrong.

Can I get a fix and flip loan with no experience?

Yes. Lenders underwrite the deal as well as the borrower, so a first-timer with strong numbers and a clear exit can be financed. Experience changes your leverage and pricing rather than your eligibility, and the lender makes the final decision.

How much money do I need to bring to my first flip?

The part of the purchase and rehab the loan does not cover, plus points, closing costs, monthly interest, and enough cash to fund the first phase of work before the first draw is released. Experienced flippers with documented deals usually bring less.

How do rehab draws work on a flip loan?

You complete a phase, submit photographs and invoices, the lender inspects, and funds are often released within a few business days. You pay for each phase first and are reimbursed, so hold cash to cover at least one phase ahead.

What credit score do I need for a fix and flip loan?

Minimums vary by lender. Our lending partners' fix and flip program starts at a 600 score. Higher scores and a documented track record improve leverage and pricing, and the lender decides each file.

What happens if my flip does not sell before the loan is due?

You ask for an extension, which is at the lender's discretion and usually costs a fee, or you refinance. Refinancing into a DSCR rental loan converts the flip into a hold, which is a setback rather than a disaster. Model both exits before you close.
