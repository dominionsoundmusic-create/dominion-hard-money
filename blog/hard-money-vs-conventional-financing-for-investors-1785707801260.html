Hard Money vs. Conventional Financing for Real Estate Investors: Which One Actually Gets Deals Done?

If you're a real estate investor, you already know that the money behind a deal matters just as much as the deal itself. The wrong financing can kill a timeline, cost you a contract, or leave you holding a property you can't move. The right financing gets you to the closing table fast and keeps your pipeline moving.

That's why understanding the real differences between hard money loans and conventional financing isn't just useful — it's essential. This isn't a conversation for homebuyers trying to pick the lowest interest rate. This is for investors who need capital that actually works in the real world of fix-and-flips, BRRRR strategies, distressed properties, and competitive markets where speed wins.

Let's break it down plainly.

What Is Hard Money Lending?

Hard money loans are short-term, asset-based loans issued by private lenders — not banks. The approval and loan terms are driven primarily by the value of the property (specifically the after-repair value, or ARV), not your personal financial profile.

Hard money lenders like Dominion Hard Money evaluate the deal: What's the property worth today? What will it be worth after renovations? Is there enough equity cushion to justify the loan? If the numbers make sense, the loan gets done — often in days, not months.

Typical hard money loan characteristics:

  • Loan terms of 6 to 24 months
  • Interest rates typically ranging from 8% to 15%
  • Loan-to-value (LTV) ratios based on ARV or purchase price
  • Minimal income verification requirements
  • Fast closings — sometimes in 5 to 10 business days
  • Points charged at origination (typically 1–4 points)

What Is Conventional Financing?

Conventional loans are issued by banks, credit unions, and mortgage companies and are underwritten according to guidelines set by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or federal agencies. They're designed for owner-occupied homes and long-term holds — not quick-turn investment deals.

To qualify for conventional financing, lenders dig deep into your financial history: credit scores, debt-to-income ratios, employment history, tax returns, and more. The property itself must also meet strict condition standards, which immediately creates problems when you're trying to finance a distressed or below-market asset.

Typical conventional loan characteristics:

  • 15 to 30-year loan terms
  • Lower interest rates (historically 5%–8% depending on market conditions)
  • Rigorous income and credit verification
  • 30 to 60+ day closing timelines
  • Property must meet minimum condition standards
  • Limited to a small number of financed investment properties

Speed: Where Hard Money Wins Outright

In competitive investment markets, speed is leverage. When a distressed property hits the market at a deep discount, the investor who can close in 10 days beats the investor who needs 45 every single time.

Conventional lenders simply cannot move that fast. Their process requires appraisals, underwriting queues, committee reviews, and layer after layer of documentation. Even if you're a perfectly qualified borrower, you're still at the mercy of that timeline.

Hard money lenders operate differently. Because the underwriting is asset-based rather than borrower-based, the review process is streamlined. Dominion Hard Money can typically review a deal and issue a term sheet within 24–48 hours. That kind of speed lets you make offers with confidence and close before your competition even gets pre-approved.

Bottom line: If you're buying off-market deals, foreclosures, auction properties, or anything that requires a fast close, hard money isn't just preferable — it's often the only option.

Property Condition: The Deal-Breaker for Conventional Loans

Here's a reality that trips up newer investors: conventional lenders won't finance properties in poor condition. Missing HVAC systems, roof damage, foundation issues, mold, or code violations can disqualify a property entirely from bank financing.

This is a significant problem because the most profitable investment deals are often the most distressed properties. The spread between purchase price and ARV is widest when a property needs serious work — and that's exactly where investors make their money.

Hard money lenders underwrite to the ARV. They're lending against what the property will be worth after you do the work, not what it looks like today. That means you can finance the acquisition of a gutted duplex, a fire-damaged single-family, or a foreclosure that no bank would touch — and use the loan to fund the rehab as well.

If your investment strategy involves value-add properties in any form, conventional financing will consistently block your deals before they get started.

Cost Comparison: Don't Let the Rate Fool You

The most common objection investors raise about hard money is the cost. And yes — hard money loans carry higher interest rates than conventional mortgages. That's not a secret, and it's not a flaw. It's a feature of what the product actually delivers.

What investors need to calculate isn't just the rate — it's the total cost of the capital relative to the return on the deal.

Consider this example:

  • You purchase a distressed property for $150,000
  • You invest $50,000 in renovations
  • The ARV is $280,000
  • You sell in 6 months for $275,000

At a 12% annual rate on a $200,000 hard money loan held for 6 months, your interest cost is approximately $12,000. Add 2 points at origination ($4,000) and your total financing cost is roughly $16,000. Against a $75,000+ gross profit, that's a cost of capital that makes complete sense.

Meanwhile, a conventional loan might have cost you the deal entirely because the property didn't qualify — or because a faster buyer beat you to it.

Use the right tool for the job. Hard money is short-term bridge capital designed to create a profitable exit, not a 30-year carry.

When Conventional Financing Does Make Sense for Investors

To be fair, conventional financing has its place in a real estate investor's toolkit — specifically for stabilized, long-term rental holds. If you're purchasing a turnkey property in good condition that you plan to rent for 10+ years, a conventional loan's lower rate and long amortization schedule can reduce your monthly carrying cost and improve cash flow.

Some investors also use a BRRRR strategy that incorporates both: hard money for the acquisition and rehab phase, followed by a cash-out refinance into a conventional loan once the property is stabilized and tenant-occupied. This approach captures the best of both — speed and flexibility on the front end, lower cost capital on the back end.

The key is knowing which product fits which phase of your investment strategy.
