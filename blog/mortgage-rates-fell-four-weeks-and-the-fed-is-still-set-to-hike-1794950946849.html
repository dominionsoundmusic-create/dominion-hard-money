Mortgage rates fell four weeks straight and the Fed is still set to hike

Published November 17, 2026 • Dominion Hard Money

Get your deal reviewedMore from the blog
← Back to Blog

The 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaged 7.11 percent in Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey for the week ending November 12, 2026, down from 7.18 percent the week before. That is the fourth straight weekly decline and the lowest reading in about seven weeks. The 15-year fixed averaged 6.45 percent in the same survey.

Now the part that matters more. A year ago the same survey read 6.24 percent. Rates are not falling. They are coming down off a higher high, and the market is already pricing the next move up. As of the November 16 close, Kalshi's contract on the December 8-9 FOMC meeting put a quarter-point hike at about 87 percent and a half-point hike at roughly 9 percent, with a hold near 4 percent and a cut at about 1 percent. The target range is 3.75 to 4.00 percent today. Traders think it goes up next month.

Long money and short money are moving apart

Those two facts are not contradictory. They describe two different borrowing costs that an investor pays at two different points in a deal.

Long-term fixed debt follows the 10-year Treasury, which sat at 5.15 percent on November 13, 2026, up from 4.11 percent a year earlier. That yield peaked at 5.31 percent on October 5, 2026, its highest close in more than two decades, and has eased since. That easing is what produced the four-week slide in the 30-year average. It is a retreat from an extreme, not a trend change.

Short-term borrowing costs are a separate animal. Floating-rate bridge, fix-and-flip and construction debt is usually indexed to a short rate that moves with the Fed. If December delivers a quarter point, the index under that paper goes up a quarter point, and it goes up on loans you have already closed.

So the split cuts against you on both ends. Your carry cost during the hold rises with the Fed. Your buyer's takeout rate, and your own refinance rate if you plan to keep the property, stays anchored above 7 percent because the long end has not come down enough to matter.

Run the exit at 7.11, not at what you hoped for

Put real numbers on it. A $300,000 loan amortized over 30 years at 7.11 percent costs $2,018 a month in principal and interest. At last year's 6.24 percent it was $1,845. That is $173 a month, or about $2,075 a year, on the same loan balance. Your retail buyer feels that difference as reduced qualifying power, which shows up in your sale price or in days on market.

On a rental hold the arithmetic is just as blunt. Add $500 a month for taxes and insurance to that $2,018 payment and you need roughly $3,022 in rent to clear a 1.20 DSCR. At last year's rate the same property cleared at about $2,814. Two hundred dollars of monthly rent is the difference between a file that funds and a file that gets re-sized or repriced. If you underwrote in the spring at a rate that assumed cuts, re-run it.

Carry cost moves the same way. Six months of interest-only on $300,000 at 10.5 percent is $15,750. At 11.5 percent it is $17,250. A single Fed hike plus a lender margin adjustment can take $1,500 straight out of a flip's profit line, and that is before extension fees.

The refinance exit is still broken

If your plan has ever been "buy it now, refinance when rates drop," look at what refinancing actually looks like. The Mortgage Bankers Association reported total application volume up 6.2 percent for the week ending November 6, 2026, with purchase applications up 8 percent and refinances up 5 percent. The refinance index was still 57 percent below the same week a year ago.

That is what a stalled refi market looks like after four weeks of improving rates. A one-week bounce off a very low base. Nobody is refinancing because almost nobody has a rate worth refinancing out of at 7 percent. Underwrite the hold on the rate you can get today, not on a rate you expect.

Demand turns on fast, and it turns off just as fast

The 8 percent weekly jump in purchase applications is the useful signal in that MBA release. Buyers are sitting on the sideline watching rate headlines, and they move within days when the number ticks down. That rate had already backed up again by mid-November, which means that demand bump likely reversed.

Practical consequence: your listing window is now tied to a rate window you cannot predict. Do not pace a rehab assuming a smooth eight-week sales period. Be finished and photographed and ready to go live, so you can list into one of these short pockets of demand instead of landing in the middle of a back-up.

What to change in your underwriting this month

  • Stress the exit rate at 7.5 percent. The 30-year average has been above 7 percent for months and peaked at 7.40 percent this fall. A 7.11 reading is the floor of the recent range, not the center of it.
  • Assume your floating index goes up a quarter point in December. Build the extra carry into the budget now. If the hike does not come, you have margin. If it does, nothing breaks.
  • Price the extension. If the sale takes two extra months at a higher index plus extension fees, is the deal still profitable? If the answer is no, the spread was too thin on day one.
  • Test DSCR at today's rate with today's insurance quote. Not last year's. Not the seller's old premium.
  • Stop modeling a cut. Futures are pricing the opposite. Any deal whose profit depends on cheaper money in 2027 is a bet on rates, not a real estate deal.

The four-week decline in the 30-year average is genuinely the best rate news since early fall. It is also worth about $14 a month on a $300,000 loan compared with where it started. Take it, but do not build a deal on it.
