Fix and Flip Loans Near Me: The Location That Actually Matters

For a flip loan, the location that counts is the house's, not yours.

Check your property's locationWhere our partners finance

A lender two states away can finance a flip on your street, but the property has to pass a set of location questions first, and one of them can change your cash to close by tens of thousands of dollars. Here are those questions, the local numbers that decide whether a flip works, and the part of your team that really does need to be nearby.

Five location questions the house has to answer

Before anyone looks closely at your credit or your rehab budget, a flip lender looks at where the property is. Every lender sets its own rules, and a house that fails one is either declined or financed on worse terms. These are the questions to ask about any address.

1. Does the lender finance in that state? Business-purpose lenders choose their states, and licensing and foreclosure rules differ from one state to the next. Ask before you make an offer, not after.

2. Does the county change the leverage? Some lenders cut leverage, for example from 70 percent of value to 50 percent, in specific counties they consider higher risk. This is the question with the biggest price tag, and it is worked through below.

3. How far is it from a real market? An appraisal needs recent comparable sales, and a resale needs buyers. Lenders commonly limit how far a property can sit from a metro area, so a remote house can be harder to finance than a similar one closer in.

4. Is the value high enough? Our lending partners' minimum loan is $50,000, and many lenders also set a floor on the finished value. In the cheapest neighborhoods and much of the rural country, finished houses can fall below it.

5. Is it a house or a piece of land? On a large lot, a house starts to be valued as land with a building on it. Lenders commonly limit acreage, so ask if the lot is big.

Pass them and the core terms follow the deal rather than the city: our lending partners lend up to 100 percent of cost or 70 percent of after-repair value, whichever binds first, at standard pricing from 12.99 percent plus 2.99 points.

One county line, $44,000 of difference

To see what a county leverage cut does, take one flip and put it in two places an hour apart, one where the lender allows its normal 70 percent of value and one where it caps leverage at 50 percent. A house costs $120,000, needs $35,000 of work, and will be worth $220,000 finished. Total cost: $155,000.

At the normal cap, seventy percent of $220,000 is $154,000, so the loan covers all but $1,000 of the cost. At standard pricing of 12.99 percent and 2.99 points, the points come to about $4,605 and interest to about $1,667 a month.

At a 50 percent cap, half of $220,000 is $110,000, so you bring $45,000 of the cost yourself. The points drop to about $3,289 and the interest to about $1,191 a month, because you are borrowing less, but your own money in the deal has gone up by $44,000.

The busiest flipping markets are exactly where this matters. ATTOM's report for the second quarter of 2026 put Canton, Ohio's flipping rate at 11.6 percent of all home sales, among the highest in the country, and Cleveland's at 10.4 percent, the highest among metros of more than a million people. If you invest in a county where your lender reduces leverage, check whether the same kind of house is available just outside the line, and ask the lender which rule applies to the exact address.

The local numbers that decide a flip

A flip is a bet on one neighborhood's resale market, and national averages hide the spread. ATTOM's flipping reports, built from recorded deeds, show how far apart local markets are.

Margins. Among metros of a million people or more, typical gross flipping margins in the second quarter of 2026 ran from 81.5 percent in Pittsburgh to a 0.3 percent loss in San Antonio. Dallas came in at 1.8 percent, Austin at 2.8 and Houston at 3.7. Gross means before renovation, financing and selling costs, which ATTOM notes can run 20 to 33 percent of a property's value, so a single-digit gross margin usually means a loss. In the big Texas metros, a flip has to be bought far below the typical discount to work.

Competition. Where flipping rates are high, you are bidding against other flippers for the same houses. The highest rates in the second quarter were in Columbus, Georgia (13.6 percent of sales), Canton, Ohio (11.6), Akron, Ohio (11.2), Fayetteville, North Carolina (10.9) and Macon, Georgia (10.6).

Cash buyers. In the first quarter of 2026, 61.1 percent of flipped homes nationally were bought with all cash. A borrower with a loan is competing against offers with no financing contingency, so having your financing lined up before you offer is part of making an offer that wins.

Pull the numbers for your own metro before you pull any house's comparables. They tell you whether your market rewards flipping at all.

Your buyer's loan shapes your sale date

The last local question is who buys the finished house, and with what loan. Federal rules on FHA-insured mortgages include a flipping restriction, 24 CFR 203.37a, and it applies to your sale, not your purchase:

Resold within 90 days of the day you acquired it, the house is not eligible for an FHA-insured loan at all.

Resold between 91 and 180 days, it is generally eligible, but if the price is 100 percent or more above what you paid, the buyer's lender must get additional documentation, including a second appraisal from a different appraiser.

How much this matters depends on where you are. ATTOM found 10.7 percent of flipped homes nationally went to FHA buyers in the second quarter of 2026, and the share varies widely from one metro to the next. Where FHA buyers are common, a fast renovation does not mean a fast sale to an FHA buyer. Count 90 days from your closing date before you set a listing date, and expect scrutiny on a big price jump before six months.

The rule is a regulation, and HUD can revise it, so confirm it is current when you plan your sale.

The part of your team that has to be local

The money can come from anywhere. These people cannot.

The contractor. Renovation money is released in draws after the work is inspected. Someone has to run the job, document progress and fix what the inspector flags, every week. If you live more than a short drive from the house, that someone is a general contractor you have checked out, with references from jobs you can go and look at.

The title company or closing attorney. Closings follow the property's state. In some states, Georgia and South Carolina among them, a real estate attorney must conduct or supervise the closing. Use one who closes investor purchases regularly and can move on a short timeline.

The agent who pulls your comparables. Your loan and your profit both rest on the after-repair value. A local agent who sells finished homes in that neighborhood will know which recent sales are true comparables and which were outliers, which an online estimate cannot tell you.

The local rules. Permits, contractor licensing and point-of-sale inspections vary by city and county. Ask the building department what a renovation of your scope needs before you close, because a permit delay is interest you pay every month.

Fix and flip loans near me: common questions

Does my fix and flip lender need to be in my state?

No. A fix and flip loan is a business-purpose loan secured by the property, and the appraisal, title work and closing all happen in the property's own market wherever the lender is based. What matters is whether the lender finances in that state and county, and on what terms.

Can I get a flip loan in any state?

Not from every lender. Business-purpose lenders choose the states they lend in, and some reduce leverage in particular counties. Send the address and the lending partner's team will confirm whether the property is eligible before you make an offer. See where our lending partners finance.

Why would my county get a smaller fix and flip loan?

Some lenders cap leverage lower, for example 50 percent of value instead of 70, in counties they see as higher risk. On a $220,000 after-repair value that is the difference between a $154,000 loan and a $110,000 loan. Ask which rule applies to the exact address.

Can I get a fix and flip loan on a rural property?

Sometimes, but it is harder. Lenders commonly limit how far a property can sit from a metro area, how large the lot can be and how low the value can go, because remote houses are harder to appraise and slower to resell. Confirm those limits with the lender for your property.

Does the FHA flipping rule affect when I can sell?

It affects who can buy. Under 24 CFR 203.37a, a home resold within 90 days of the seller's purchase is not eligible for an FHA-insured mortgage. Between 91 and 180 days, a resale at double the purchase price or more requires a second appraisal. In markets where many buyers use FHA loans, list with that calendar in mind.

Do I need to live near my flip?

No, but someone you trust does. Renovation money is released in draws after the work is inspected, so a flipper who lives far away needs a general contractor who can run the job and document progress. If you cannot visit the house every week, price that oversight into your budget.
