What a Rental Really Costs to Hold, State by State

The same $400,000 rental costs thousands more to hold on one side of a state line than the other.

What does a rental property actually cost to hold?

Far more than the mortgage payment, and the gap is wider than most investors budget for. On a $400,000 rental, the difference between two Connecticut towns on this page is over $7,000 a year in property tax alone, before insurance, before maintenance, before a single vacancy.

Most rental analysis stops at principal, interest, taxes and insurance, with taxes estimated at "about one percent" and insurance treated as a rounding error. In four of the twelve states below that assumption is roughly right. In the other eight it is wrong by the whole margin: enough to turn a deal that pencils into one that bleeds.

The pattern that matters: every state has one line item that behaves nothing like the national average, and it is a different line in each state. Florida's is insurance. Texas's is property tax. Massachusetts's is neither: it is legal exposure that never appears in a cap rate at all. Before you buy in a state you have not bought in before, find out which line is the outlier there, then get a real number for it.

The table below runs one hypothetical property, a $400,000 single-family rental, three bedrooms, reasonable condition, rented at market, through twelve states, and names the single line item that decides deals in each.

This is a structural comparison, not a shopping list. A $400,000 house is a modest property in Boston and an expensive one in Cleveland. The point is to show how differently the same money behaves depending on which side of a state line it sits.

Which states have a property tax problem?

Texas, Connecticut, Michigan and Ohio, each for a different reason, and only one of them is the headline rate.

Texas charges you for the missing income tax every month. At roughly 1.40% effective statewide that is $467 a month on a $400,000 house before anything else. Texas appraises at market value, and the protest process is a real lever rather than a formality: you can argue value and unequal appraisal together, and the deadline is generally May 15 or 30 days after your notice is mailed, whichever is later.

Connecticut cannot be answered with a state-level number at all. Most towns assess at 70% of value and tax at whatever the town's mill rate happens to be. Hartford is the exception: its 69.95 mills, the highest in the state, applies to one to three family homes assessed at 36.75% of value, so the same house costs about $10,280 a year there against $2,835 in Greenwich, a spread of about $7,450. Check which assessment ratio applies before you multiply by a mill rate. County-level guidance is worse than useless: Connecticut abolished county government in 1960, and Fairfield County contains both Bridgeport at 27.95 mills, after a large cut for fiscal 2027, and Greenwich at 10.125. Mill rates are reset every year, so check the current one for the town, not last year's.

Michigan is misunderstood in both directions. The exemption a rental loses is the Principal Residence Exemption, which is a different thing from the Homestead Property Tax Credit, and it exempts a principal residence from up to 18 mills of local school operating millage. Michigan also taxes taxable value, whose annual growth is capped, rather than market value or state equalized value, which breaks most out-of-state underwriting.

Ohio is gentler than its rates look. House Bill 920 tax reduction factors cut effective rates on voted levies when values rise, so a big jump in county valuations does not produce a matching jump in bills. House Bill 126, effective in 2022, sharply restricted school boards' power to challenge property values, a tool that once chased recently sold homes upward, which makes Ohio friendlier to buyers than most summaries suggest.

Which states have an insurance problem?

Florida and Colorado most clearly, and in Florida the insurance line can rival the entire annual property tax bill in other states on this list.

Florida premiums run at several times the national average for the same coverage, and published statewide averages differ so much by source and county that only a quote on the actual house is worth underwriting. Florida also has a non-homestead assessment cap of 10% a year, which resets on a change of ownership and is lost when an improvement raises just value by 25% or more. The recapture rule means assessed value can keep climbing even when market value falls, which bites in markets that have softened.

Texas adds a second exposure along the coast: price any flood policy the property needs, separately from the homeowner policy, before you offer.

Colorado is widely described as a wildfire market. Colorado Division of Insurance data put hail at 26% to 54% of homeowner premiums depending on the county, and property insurance costs rose an average of 57.9% from 2018 to 2023. Wildfire shows up more as an availability problem: it can get homes nonrenewed rather than merely made expensive.

One thing worth knowing before you underwrite anywhere. Property tax is the only major carrying cost that a buyer can influence after closing. Every state on this page has an appeal process, and the deadlines are short: Florida gives 25 days from the mailing of the TRIM notice, Georgia 45 days from the date on the assessment notice, Texas generally until May 15. Missing the window costs a full year.

Which states have a legal problem rather than a cost problem?

Massachusetts and Maryland, where the risk is not that the numbers are high but that the property cannot legally earn a dollar until the paperwork clears.

Maryland gates the tenancy behind lead registration and, in Baltimore City, a rental license. That means it gates the income. A new owner must register a pre-1978 rental with the Maryland Department of the Environment within 30 days of acquiring it, and from January 1, 2026 a Baltimore City rental license no longer transfers on a sale: the buyer must apply for a new one within 60 days.

Massachusetts carries two separate exposures. The Lead Law imposes strict liability where a pre-1978 property is home to a child under six, until lead hazards are removed or covered and the owner holds a Letter of Compliance. Interim control is the practical middle path: a Letter of Interim Control is valid for one year and can be recertified for one more.

On security deposits, the common statement of the rule is broader than the law. It is often said that any violation triggers automatic treble damages. The Supreme Judicial Court held otherwise in Phillips v. Equity Residential Management, 478 Mass. 251 (2017): paperwork violations, such as a defective itemized list of deductions, do not carry treble damages. The failures that do are the ones that suggest the money was never held properly: not keeping it in a separate account, not returning it within thirty days of the tenancy ending, and not transferring it when the property is sold. That last one catches investors at closing.

Questions investors ask

What percentage of rent should I budget for expenses on a rental?

The fifty percent rule is a starting point, not an answer, and it fails hardest in exactly the states where the money is. In Florida the insurance line alone can eat a large share of gross rent. In Hartford the tax line can do the same. Build the number from actual line items for the specific state rather than applying a national ratio.

Why is my property tax higher than the previous owner's?

Usually one of three mechanisms. In Michigan the property lost its Principal Residence Exemption when it stopped being owner-occupied, adding up to 18 mills of school operating tax. In Florida the non-homestead assessment cap reset on the change of ownership, so you are taxed on current value rather than the seller's capped basis. In Pennsylvania, a sale can prompt a taxing district appeal that resets the assessment.

Which state is best for buying rental property?

There is no single answer. Massachusetts and Maryland carry the heaviest compliance burden on this list. Texas and Connecticut's high-rate towns carry the heaviest tax. Florida and Colorado carry the heaviest insurance. The right question is not which state is best but which line item in a given state you are equipped to manage.

Does Washington's rent cap apply to my single-family rental?

Usually, yes, if you are an investor. The exemptions cover units within twelve years of their first certificate of occupancy, certain owner-occupied properties, and some nonprofit and public housing, not investor-owned single-family homes generally.
