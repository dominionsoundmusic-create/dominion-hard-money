Decide which Virginia you are buying before you pick a house

Most states have expensive corners and cheap ones. Virginia has three distinct investment markets, and a statewide median describes none of them.

Hampton Roads (Norfolk, Virginia Beach, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Newport News and Hampton) has the lower entry prices and the higher rent-to-price ratios, which is why it is where a DSCR ratio usually clears most easily.

Richmond sits between the two on price, with a mix of older city neighborhoods and newer suburban stock in Henrico and Chesterfield counties.

Northern Virginia (Fairfax, Arlington, Loudoun and Prince William) carries the state's highest prices and household incomes. At today's prices it is mostly a price-level and appreciation market, not one where a rental pays well on day one.

Both strategies are legitimate. The mistake is underwriting one as if it were the other.

Who actually pays the rent here

Virginia's rental demand leans on the federal government and the military more than almost any other state. In Hampton Roads it is the Navy, with Naval Station Norfolk as the anchor, plus the shipyards and the port. In Northern Virginia it is federal employment and the contractors around it.

The upside is real: housing allowances put a floor under rents, and demand does not hinge on one private employer. The concentration is real too. Two of the state's biggest rental markets depend on federal budgets, and military tenants move on orders, so turnover follows the permanent-change-of-station calendar rather than your lease term. Put that in the vacancy and make-ready lines rather than in your optimism.

What a Virginia flip pays at closing

Virginia charges two separate taxes when a deed is recorded. The recordation tax, customarily paid by the buyer, is 25 cents per $100 of value at the state level, and cities and counties may add up to a third of that. The grantor tax, paid by the seller, is 50 cents per $500, or 0.1 percent. Localities in Northern Virginia and Hampton Roads also add a regional congestion relief fee to the grantor side.

On a flip you pay the buyer's side when you buy and the seller's side when you sell. A deed of trust securing the loan is also subject to recordation tax, so the hard money loan and any later refinance each add a line to the closing statement. None of these is large on its own; together on a thin flip they matter.

Property tax is set by each city and county as a rate per $100 of assessed value, with assessments at full market value and reassessed annually in many localities. A rental pays the same rate as an owner-occupied home, but check the locality's current rate and whether the purchase will trigger a new assessment.

Foreclosure and landlord law in Virginia

Foreclosure is usually nonjudicial. Most Virginia loans are secured by a deed of trust, and the trustee sells after the required notice and advertisement without a court case. There is no right to redeem after the sale, so title at a trustee's sale is final once the sale closes and the deed is recorded.

Landlord rules. Most residential rentals fall under the Virginia Residential Landlord and Tenant Act. A security deposit may not exceed two months' rent and must be returned, with an itemized list of deductions, within 45 days after the tenancy ends. Nonpayment cases start with a written pay-or-quit notice and then go to general district court; the notice periods and tenant protections have been amended several times in recent sessions, so use current forms.

Rental inspections. Virginia law lets localities create rental inspection districts and require inspections of rentals within them. Several cities do, so check the address before you budget the make-ready.

Rent control. Virginia has not allowed local rent control, though proposals to change that have come up in recent legislative sessions. Check current law before you underwrite rent growth in a multifamily deal.

The markets, in the order most investors should look at them

Richmond: the all-round market

Prices well below Northern Virginia, a large university and state government employment base, and a choice of neighborhoods that lets you pick your risk: older city stock in Church Hill, Manchester and the Northside with renovation upside and management intensity to match, and suburban Henrico and Chesterfield with newer houses and longer tenancies.

Hampton Roads: the yield end

The easiest place in the state to build DSCR coverage. Model it honestly: higher turnover from military moves, older housing in parts of Norfolk and Portsmouth, and flood exposure in low-lying neighborhoods, where a flood zone check and a flood insurance quote belong in every file.

Northern Virginia: price level, not cash flow

High prices against rents mean few properties cash flow at market prices. The case is structural: constrained land, high incomes and a deep employment base. Buy it knowingly.

Roanoke, Lynchburg and the smaller markets

The cheapest entry points in the state, with less liquidity on the exit, which matters more on a flip than on a hold.

Who pays the recordation tax in Virginia?

By custom the buyer pays the recordation tax (25 cents per $100 at the state level, plus any local amount) and the seller pays the grantor tax (0.1 percent, plus a regional fee in Northern Virginia and Hampton Roads). On a flip you pay one going in and the other coming out.

Norfolk or Richmond for a first Virginia rental?

Hampton Roads if the goal is DSCR coverage and cash flow, since prices are lower relative to rent. Richmond if you want a balance of yield and a liquid resale market.

Is there a redemption period after a Virginia foreclosure?

No. Most Virginia foreclosures are trustee sales under a deed of trust, and there is no right to redeem after the sale.

How much security deposit can a Virginia landlord charge?

Up to two months' rent under the Virginia Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, returned with an itemized list of deductions within 45 days after the tenancy ends.
