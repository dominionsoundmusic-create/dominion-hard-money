Kentucky's investor markets

Kentucky is a good state for an investor who knows its quirks. Houses cost less than the national average in much of the state, the transfer tax is small, and the big markets are active. But its landlord law changes from county to county, and its foreclosure rules can give an owner a second chance after the sale. Here is how both work, and what our lending partners offer.

Louisville and Jefferson County are the largest market, with the ring of suburbs from Shelbyville to Elizabethtown. Lexington and the Bluegrass counties around it, Georgetown, Nicholasville, Richmond and Winchester, are the second. Northern Kentucky, Covington, Newport and Florence in Boone, Kenton and Campbell counties, trades as part of the Cincinnati metro. Bowling Green, an hour from Nashville, and the river cities, Ashland with Huntington, Henderson with Evansville, borrow buyers from their neighbors.

The eastern coalfield is the hard case. Towns such as Hazard and Pikeville have few recent comparable sales and a thin pool of buyers for a finished house, and private lenders commonly look harder at that kind of location. Ask about the exact address before you count on financing there.

Kentucky's landlord law depends on the county

This is the Kentucky fact most out-of-state investors miss. The state has a Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, but under KRS 383.500 it applies only where a city, county or urban county government has adopted it, and it must be adopted in its entirety. Jefferson County (Louisville) and Fayette County (Lexington) have adopted it, as have Oldham and Pulaski counties and a list of cities that includes Covington, Newport, Florence, Georgetown and Shelbyville. The list changes, so confirm it with the city or county for the address you are buying.

Inside an adopting area, the statute sets the rules: a seven-day written notice for unpaid rent, a fourteen-day notice with a chance to cure for other material breaches, specific handling of security deposits, and a statutory duty on the landlord to maintain the property.

Outside one, the lease, Kentucky's forcible detainer statutes and common law govern instead. Kentucky's Supreme Court held in Miles v. Shauntee (1983) that the state has no common-law implied warranty of habitability, so outside the act the lease does far more of the work.

For a buy-and-hold investor, this changes how you write leases and how long a problem tenant takes to resolve. Use a lease written for the rule set that applies. Kentucky also bars cities and counties from controlling rents on private property under KRS 65.875, so the rent is set by the market wherever you buy.

What the state adds to your costs

Property tax. Kentucky assesses real property at full cash value. The state's own rate for 2026 is 10.3 cents per $100 of assessed value, down from 10.6 cents in 2025, the sixth straight annual cut, because state law holds the growth in state real property revenue to 4 percent a year. On a $200,000 house that state portion is about $206. The county, city, school and special district rates stacked on top are most of the bill and vary widely, so get the actual rates for the property's district from the county property valuation administrator before you estimate holding costs.

Transfer tax. Under KRS 142.050 the tax is 50 cents per $500 of value, 0.1 percent, and the statute places it on the grantor, the seller. On a flip you would normally pay it when you sell: about $250 on a $250,000 resale, unless the contract shifts it. It is small next to many neighboring states.

Foreclosure goes through the courts, with a redemption window. Kentucky forecloses judicially, through a master commissioner's sale. Under KRS 426.530, if the property sells for less than two-thirds of its appraised value, the owner can redeem it within six months of the sale by paying the price plus 10 percent interest a year. For an investor buying at a commissioner's sale, that is a reason to bid with the appraisal in mind; for a lender, it is one more reason to look hard at the exit plan.

Can I get a hard money loan for a flip in Kentucky?

Yes. Our lending partners review non-owner-occupied investment property in Kentucky held in an LLC or corporation, most often in Louisville, Lexington, Northern Kentucky and Bowling Green. In rural eastern Kentucky, ask about the exact address early. The lender decides each deal.

Does Kentucky's landlord-tenant act apply everywhere in the state?

No. Under KRS 383.500 it applies only in cities and counties that have adopted it, in full. Jefferson and Fayette counties are among those that have. Elsewhere, the lease, the forcible detainer statutes and common law govern.

What is the property tax on a rental in Kentucky?

The state portion for 2026 is 10.3 cents per $100 of assessed value. County, city, school and special district rates are added on top and make up most of the bill, so check with the property valuation administrator for the county where the house sits.

Who pays the transfer tax in Kentucky?

KRS 142.050 places the tax, 50 cents per $500 of value, on the seller. On a flip you would normally pay it at the resale, about $250 on a $250,000 sale, unless your contract says otherwise.

Can a Kentucky owner get the house back after a foreclosure sale?

Sometimes. If the property sells for less than two-thirds of its appraised value, KRS 426.530 lets the owner redeem it within six months by paying the sale price plus 10 percent annual interest.

Does Kentucky allow rent control?

No. KRS 65.875 reserves rent control to the General Assembly, so cities and counties cannot cap rents on private property.
