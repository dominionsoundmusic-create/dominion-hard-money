What lenders ask of a first-time flipper

Most flip lenders say they finance beginners, and our lending partners do. The difference is in what they ask of a borrower with no finished flips.

Less leverage. Lenders publish experience tiers. A typical example: a borrower with no completed flips can borrow around 85 percent of the purchase price and 100 percent of the rehab, capped at 70 percent of after-repair value, while a borrower with several flips can borrow 90 percent of the purchase. Our lending partners lend up to 100 percent of cost and 70 percent of after-repair value, whichever limit binds first, and how much of that a borrower gets depends on the experience they can document over the past three years.

Standard pricing. Our lending partners' standard pricing starts at 12.99 percent interest plus 2.99 points. Loyalty pricing of 10.99 percent plus 1.99 points is available only after two loans have been paid off with the lending partner in good standing, so a first-time borrower should plan on standard pricing and earn the way down.

Reserves. Expect the lender to ask for cash in the bank beyond what you bring to closing, shown on statements. Ask how much it wants for your deal before you make an offer. It is there for the week the contractor finds rot under the bathroom floor.

A renovation a beginner can manage. Lenders look harder at a big rehab in the hands of someone who has never run one, and some limit the scope or the budget for new investors. Confirm any renovation limit with the lender for your deal before you commit to a gut job.

A floor on the loan. Our lending partners' minimum loan is $50,000 and minimum credit score is 600. Very cheap houses can fall below what any lender will finance.

None of these is a penalty. Together they describe the kind of first deal that tends to survive: a modest house, a cosmetic renovation, cash in reserve and a margin wide enough to absorb a mistake.

What the 2026 numbers say about where beginners should buy

ATTOM's home flipping reports, drawn from recorded sales deeds, give a beginner three things worth knowing before choosing a house.

The price band matters more than the city. In the first quarter of 2026, flipped homes bought for $100,000 to $200,000 produced the strongest typical margin nationally, 32 percent. Homes bought for under $50,000 typically lost 14 percent. The cheapest house on the street is often cheap for a reason the renovation cannot fix.

"Profit" in the headlines is gross. In the second quarter of 2026 the typical flip made $60,526 between purchase and resale, a 21.5 percent return, down from 25.7 percent the quarter before. ATTOM's figure leaves out the renovation, the financing and the selling costs, which it notes can run 20 to 33 percent of a property's value. Run that on a $150,000 house flipped at the typical margin: it resells for about $182,250, a gross of about $32,250, while 20 to 33 percent of the resale price is roughly $36,500 to $60,100. At the national average, a beginner paying standard rates loses money. You need a better-than-average buy.

Some big markets barely work at all right now. Among metros of a million people or more, the smallest typical margins in the second quarter were in San Antonio (a 0.3 percent loss), Dallas (1.8 percent), Austin (2.8) and Houston (3.7). On gross numbers that thin, the typical flip in those cities did not cover its own costs. The widest were in Pittsburgh (81.5 percent), Buffalo (76.6), New Orleans (75), Virginia Beach (63.4) and Philadelphia (62.8). Wide margins attract competition, and lenders set their own rules by market, so check the terms for the specific address before you count on full leverage.

A first flip, worked to the dollar

Here is a deal a first-time borrower could realistically get financed. A three-bedroom house in need of cosmetic work costs $150,000. The renovation is budgeted at $35,000, under a quarter of the price. Recent sales of finished comparable homes support an after-repair value of $250,000.

The loan. Our lending partners lend up to 100 percent of cost but no more than 70 percent of after-repair value. Seventy percent of $250,000 is $175,000, below the $185,000 total cost, so the loan is at most $175,000 and you bring the other $10,000. With no track record, the lender may offer less, so ask before you count on the maximum.

The cost of the money. Points at 2.99 percent: about $5,230 at closing. Interest at 12.99 percent, paid monthly on an interest-only loan: about $1,894 a month. At the national typical 161 days in the second quarter of 2026, a little over five months, that is about $10,030.

Everything else. Assume buying costs of about 2 percent of the price ($3,000), taxes, insurance and utilities of about $400 a month while you hold it ($2,100), and commissions and seller closing costs of about 7 percent of the sale ($17,500). Your market's figures will differ; plug them in.

What is left. $250,000 sale, minus $150,000 purchase, $35,000 renovation, $5,230 points, $10,030 interest, $3,000, $2,100 and $17,500, leaves about $27,100. Every cost after the purchase came to about 29 percent of the resale price, inside ATTOM's 20 to 33 percent range.

What you needed in cash. The $10,000 gap, the $5,230 in points and the $3,000 in buying costs: about $18,230 at closing. Then whatever reserves the lender asks to see, shown, not spent. Then the monthly interest until the sale.

The same deal, the way first flips usually go

First flips rarely run to plan. Keep the same house and let two ordinary things happen: the renovation runs 15 percent over budget, another $5,250 from your pocket because the loan does not grow with the overrun, and the sale takes three months longer, about $6,880 more in interest and holding costs. The profit drops from about $27,100 to about $15,000.

That is still a profit, and it is why the cautious terms above exist. A modest renovation keeps the overrun small. Reserves cover it. A wide buy leaves room to be wrong. Now shrink the buy: had this deal been bought at the national typical margin of 21.5 percent rather than at a 67 percent spread between purchase and resale, the same two slips would have turned it into a clear loss.

The timeline deserves its own warning. Ask the lender for the loan term and the extension terms in writing before you close. An extension is at the lender's discretion and usually costs a fee, so plan the work and the sale to finish well inside the original term.

Getting ready before you make an offer

Form the LLC first. Our lending partners lend to an entity, an LLC or corporation, and owners are commonly asked to sign for the loan personally, so the LLC organizes the deal rather than shielding you from it. Setting one up takes days to weeks depending on your state and should not be the thing that delays your closing.

Put the reserves somewhere you can show them. Recent statements for the account holding your reserves and your closing cash are a common ask. Money that just arrived needs a paper trail.

Get a real bid before you count on a number. A $35,000 budget should come from a contractor who has walked the house, itemized by room, not from an online estimator. Renovation money is typically released in draws against that scope, after the work is inspected.

Pull the comparables yourself. The after-repair value sets both your loan and your profit. Use three or more recent sales of finished homes nearby, of similar size and age, and be suspicious of any one that makes your deal look better than the rest.

Know your credit history. The program's minimum score is 600. A recent bankruptcy, foreclosure or short sale can make a borrower ineligible, and each lender sets its own look-back period. Check your report before an application finds a problem for you.

Stay out of it as a home. Fix and flip loans are business-purpose loans on non-owner-occupied property. If you or a family member plan to live in the house, this is the wrong loan.

Where a first flip goes after the renovation

Not every first flip should be sold. If the renovated house would rent well, a flip can become a rental by refinancing the short-term loan into a long-term DSCR loan sized on the rent, which returns much of your cash and keeps the property. It is a different set of numbers, worked through in our guide to getting a rental with little money down. Decide which exit you want before you buy, because it changes which house makes sense.

Can I get a fix and flip loan with no experience?

Yes. Our lending partners lend to first-time investors, and no completed flips are required. What credit score does a beginner need for a fix and flip loan?

Our lending partners' fix and flip program starts at a 600 score. Lenders also look for recent credit events such as a bankruptcy, foreclosure or short sale, and each sets its own look-back period, so pull your report before you apply.

How much money do I need for my first flip?

More than the down payment. You need the part of the purchase and renovation the loan does not cover, the points, closing costs, the monthly interest while you work, and the reserves the lender wants to see. On the $150,000 house worked on this page, that is about $18,230 at closing, plus reserves, plus about $1,894 a month in interest.

Is there a limit on the rehab budget for a first-time flipper?

Often, yes. Lenders look harder at large renovations by new investors, and some cap the budget or the scope until you have completed deals. Confirm any limit with the lender for your specific deal. A cosmetic renovation on a sound house is the easiest first flip to finance and the hardest to lose money on.
