What a hard money lender actually does

A hard money lender provides short-term financing for real estate investment deals. The loan is secured by the property itself, not by the borrower's income, employment, or debt-to-income ratio. That is the fundamental difference from a bank: the property is the collateral, and the deal's numbers are what gets it funded or declined.

Hard money lenders serve a specific market: investors who need to close fast, who are buying properties that need work, or whose financial profile does not fit into the conventional lending box. The typical loan is 6 to 12 months, interest-only, with the principal repaid when the investor sells the property or refinances into a long-term loan.

What a lender checks before funding

  • The property's current value. An appraiser or the lender's internal valuation team determines the as-is value. This sets the baseline for the loan-to-value ratio.
  • The after-repair value (ARV). For fix-and-flip and rehab deals, the lender evaluates what the property will be worth after the planned improvements. The ARV determines how much the lender will advance, including rehab funds.
  • The loan-to-value ratio. Most hard money lenders cap at 65 to 75 percent of the as-is value, or 70 to 85 percent of ARV. The lower the LTV, the less risk the lender takes and the better the terms you get.
  • The exit strategy. How does the loan get repaid? Selling the property, refinancing into a DSCR rental loan, or refinancing into a conventional mortgage. A clear, realiztic exit is what separates a fundable deal from a speculative one.
  • Borrower experience. First-time investors can get funded, but a borrower with 5 or 10 completed deals gets better rates, higher leverage, and faster processing. Most lenders track this explicitly.
  • Credit score. Most require a minimum of 600 to 650. The score is not the deciding factor: it is a threshold. Above the minimum, the property and the deal carry the weight.
  • Location. The property needs to be within 45 to 60 minutes of a metro area of at least 200,000 people. Rural properties and small towns are generally outside the lender's comfort zone because they are harder to liquidate if the borrower defaults.

Questions to ask before you sign

These are the questions that separate a lender who will fund your deal from one who will not, and the ones that protect you from terms you did not expect.

  • Are you the lender or a broker? A direct lender makes the funding decision. A broker submits your deal to someone else. Both can work. What matters is knowing who is making the call, because that affects speed, certainty, and who to call when something goes wrong.
  • What are the total costs? Interest rate, origination points, appraisal fee, processing fee, legal fee, draw inspection fee, extension fee. Get every number on paper before committing. The interest rate is not the cost: the total cost is the cost.
  • How are rehab draws handled? Most lenders hold rehab funds in escrow and release them in draws as work is completed and inspected. Ask how many draws are included, how long each draw takes to fund after inspection, and whether there is a cost per draw.
  • What happens if I need an extension? Deals take longer than planned. Ask what an extension costs, how long it adds, and whether it requires re-approval. Some lenders charge a flat fee; others charge additional points.
  • What are the prepayment terms? Some loans have a minimum interest period: you pay three or six months of interest even if you sell the property in two. Ask whether there is a prepayment penalty and how it is calculated.
  • What triggers a default? Late payment, insurance lapse, failure to complete rehab on schedule, change of use: know what puts you in default before you sign, because the cure provisions and the penalties are different for each.

Red flags in a hard money lender

  • Guaranteed approval before seeing the deal. No legitimate lender approves a loan without evaluating the property, the numbers, and the borrower. If someone promises funding before asking any questions, they are selling something other than a loan.
  • Large upfront fees before a commitment letter. A reasonable application or appraisal fee is normal. Thousands of dollars before the lender has committed to anything is not.
  • Refusal to put terms in writing. If the rate, the points, the fees, and the draw process only exist in a phone conversation, you have nothing enforceable. A real lender issues a term sheet.
  • Vague answers about the capital source. You deserve to know where the money comes from. A fund, an individual, a line of credit: the source affects your risk and your recourse.
  • Pressure to skip title insurance or an appraisal. Both protect you. A lender who discourages them is cutting corners.

Direct lender vs. broker

A direct hard money lender funds from their own capital or a fund they manage. The underwriting decision happens in-house, which usually means faster processing and a single point of contact.

A broker connects you with a lending partner and earns a fee for placing the deal. The advantage is access: a good broker knows multiple lenders and can match your deal to the one with the best terms for your situation. The trade-off is one more step in the chain.

Frequently asked questions

What does a hard money lender look at before funding?

The property value (as-is and after repair), the loan-to-value ratio, the borrower's exit strategy, credit score (usually 600-650 minimum), experience, and the property's location. The property and the deal carry more weight than the borrower's income.

How do I know if a hard money lender is legitimate?

Ask for a written term sheet before paying anything. Verify the lender is registered where they lend. Ask who the capital source is. Check for reviews and references from other investors. Be cautious of anyone who guarantees approval before seeing the deal.

What should a hard money term sheet include?

Interest rate, origination points, term length, extension options, draw schedule, prepayment terms, default provisions, and all fees. If any are missing or only verbal, ask for them in writing.

What is the difference between a hard money lender and a broker?

A direct lender funds from their own capital. A broker connects you with a lender and earns a placement fee. Both can get you funded: ask which one you are dealing with so you know who makes the underwriting call.

How much does a hard money lender charge?

Typical pricing is 10 to 14 percent interest with 2 to 4 origination points. Rates improve with experience: a borrower with completed deals pays less than a first-timer on the same property.

Can a hard money lender fund in all 50 states?

Most national lenders cover 40 to 47 states. Some states have licensing restrictions. Always confirm your state is covered before applying.
