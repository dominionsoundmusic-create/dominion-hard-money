Hard Money Lenders in Louisville: More Houses to Choose From, Fewer Excuses for Thin Margins

Louisville inventory has jumped while prices have held flat. Here is what that means for a flip, plus the city rules that catch investors: the rental registry, Landbank conditions and historic districts.

A market that finally gives buyers time

For several years, investors searching for hard money lenders in Louisville were competing for a handful of listings. That has changed. In June 2026 the Greater Louisville Association of REALTORS counted 4,193 homes for sale across its region, up 35.4 percent from 3,096 a year earlier. Supply rose from 2.5 months to 3.3 months, and the average home took 47 days to sell instead of 38.

Prices did not follow inventory up. The June median was $304,400, almost exactly the $305,000 of June 2025, and sellers received 98.4 percent of their list price on average. Closings were up 10.5 percent to 1,638, but pending sales were down 13.9 percent, which hints that the next few months may be slower.

For a flipper that cuts both ways. You have more houses to choose from and more room to negotiate. You also cannot count on rising prices to rescue a thin margin, and your finished house will sit next to more competing listings than it would have two years ago. Underwrite your resale at today's comparable sales, not at a hoped-for number, and give yourself a realistic number of months on the market when you plan how long you will carry short-term money.

Register the rental, even if you just bought it

Louisville Metro runs a rental registry under Chapter 119 of its code. Any property occupied, or offered for long-term occupancy, in exchange for rent has to be registered with the Department of Codes and Regulations. Short-term rentals, hotels and an accessory dwelling unit on an owner-occupied lot are among the exemptions.

The detail that catches investors is that a registration does not transfer with the property. When you buy a house that already has a tenant, the seller's registration does not cover you. You register again in your own name, or your LLC's, with the owner's contact details and the details of any property manager. If the owner is a company, the city wants the name and contact details of a responsible person.

The fee depends on zoning: $25 for R-4 and R-5 lots, $50 for R-5A through R-7 and several commercial zones, $100 for R-8 and OR-2, and $250 for OR-3, C-2 and C-3. The penalty for skipping it is far larger. The city can fine up to $100 a day for each housing unit, and an owner with an unregistered property is subject to inspection of all of that owner's rental units under the Property Maintenance Code.

Put the registration on your closing checklist, next to the insurance binder and the utility transfers.

The $100,000 floor and the cheaper side of town

Our lending partner will not finance a property whose after-repair value is under $100,000. In a metro where the median sale is around $300,000 that sounds like a formality, but Louisville is not one price. The median blends suburban houses in the east of the county with older, smaller houses closer to the river, and some blocks in the older city still trade for well under six figures even after a full renovation.

So the first question on a cheap Louisville house is not the purchase price. It is what the house will be worth when the work is done, backed by recent sales of renovated homes on similar streets. If those sales sit below $100,000, the deal falls outside the program however good the discount looks. If they sit just above it, keep in mind the 70 percent cap: on a $110,000 after-repair value the loan cannot exceed $77,000, purchase and rehab combined, and the loan minimum is $50,000.

When you send a deal, include three or four renovated sales near the address. It is the fastest way to show the after-repair value is real.

Dollar houses from the Landbank come with conditions

Louisville Metro's Landbank Authority sells vacant structures and lots it has acquired, and some of the prices are famous. Under its Save Our Structures program, a house that would otherwise be a demolition candidate can sell for $1. Demo for Deed also charges $1, but the buyer must demolish the structure within 60 days of the sale. Listings are posted on the city's online property portal, and applications are reviewed in the order received, with a seven-day window for competing proposals.

Read the conditions before you think about financing:

  • Save Our Structures requires proof that you have the money or financing for the structural and exterior repairs, an itemized budget and a 12-month timeline. The city's program page also lists primary-residence use and a five-year no-transfer period among the restrictions, so confirm in writing what the deed will allow before you plan a flip or a rental.
  • Homeowners First sells houses at land value to buyers who will live in them as a main home for five years. It is not for rentals, and it does not fit investment-property financing, which is for property you will not live in.
  • Build Back Our Blocks sells vacant lots for $500 or $1,000 depending on size, to buyers with plans and funds to build.

A $1 purchase price does not change the lending math. The loan is still sized on cost and capped at 70 percent of after-repair value, and the $100,000 after-repair floor still applies. What a dollar house really changes is your cash needs: nearly all of the cost is rehab, and the lender will want to see the repair budget, the timeline and how you will meet the city's deadline.

Historic districts add a review step

Louisville has eight local preservation districts: Butchertown, Cherokee Triangle, Chickasaw, Clifton, Limerick, Old Louisville, Parkland and West Main Street. Inside them, exterior changes generally need a Certificate of Appropriateness, approved either by city landmarks staff or by an architectural review committee against the district's design guidelines. Old Louisville has been a preservation district since 1974.

That matters for a rehab budget. New windows, siding, porch rebuilds and roof work can need approval before you start, and the materials the guidelines call for can cost more than the cheapest option. Check the address on the city's LOJIC map during your inspection period, and build the review time into your schedule. A lender reviewing your timeline will ask how long the work takes, and an approval you have not applied for yet is part of that answer.

What you will pay at closing and while you hold

Transfer tax is small. Kentucky's deed tax is 50 cents for each $500 of value, and the statute places it on the grantor, the seller. On a flip you normally pay it when you sell: $230 on a $230,000 resale, unless your contract says otherwise.

Property tax depends on where in Metro the house sits. Louisville and Jefferson County share one Metro government, but the old city limits still matter for tax. On the 2025 final rate schedule from the Jefferson County Property Valuation Administrator, the state levy was 10.6 cents per $100, Metro 11.8 cents and the JCPS school levy 71.7 cents. Inside the Urban Services District, whose boundary matches the former City of Louisville, another 31.9 cents applied. Those four lines alone come to $1.26 per $100, or $2,520 a year on a $200,000 assessment, before any other levy. Outside that district the 31.9 cents does not apply, but a parcel inside one of the county's fire districts pays that district's levy. The state's own rate fell to 10.3 cents for 2026. Get the full bill for the exact parcel from the PVA before you size a rental's payment.

Your assessment can change after you renovate. Kentucky values property at its fair cash value as of January 1. In 2026 the Jefferson County PVA reassessed residential property in Areas 8 and 9, about 54,500 parcels in eastern neighborhoods such as Anchorage, Hurstbourne, Lyndon, Middletown and Prospect. Values are based on recent arm's-length sales, and renovated homes are compared with other renovated homes. If you disagree with a value, the first step is a conference with the PVA during the inspection period; in 2026 the online window ran from April 24 to May 18. After that, an appeal goes to the Local Board of Assessment Appeals, filed through the county clerk, and then to the Kentucky Board of Tax Appeals.

A worked Louisville flip

Say you buy a house for $120,000, budget $50,000 for the work and expect it to sell for $230,000 when finished.

  • Total cost: $170,000.
  • The 70 percent cap: 70 percent of $230,000 is $161,000, which is lower than your cost, so $161,000 is the most the loan can be. You bring the other $9,000, plus closing costs.
  • Points: 2.99 points on $161,000 is about $4,814.
  • Interest: at 12.99 percent, interest only, about $1,743 a month while the full $161,000 is outstanding.
  • Reserves: generally at least $15,000 shown at closing, on top of what you put in.

If the house takes six months to finish and sell, the interest alone is around $10,457. That is why the slower 2026 market matters: two extra months on the market is roughly another $3,486. Run your own numbers with the hard money calculator.

Is hard money lending legal in Kentucky?

Yes. Private business-purpose lending secured by investment property is legal in Kentucky, and under KRS 360.010 parties can agree in writing to any interest rate on a loan whose original principal is over $15,000. The lender, not Dominion Hard Money, makes and funds the loan.

Where do hard money lenders get their money?

It varies by lender, and it is a fair question to ask any lender you talk to. Dominion Hard Money does not lend its own funds; it arranges financing through a third-party lending partner, and that lender decides and funds every loan.

Can I get a hard money loan on a cheap Louisville house?

Only if the after-repair value is at least $100,000. Our lending partner does not finance properties below that, and the loan is capped at 70 percent of the after-repair value. Back your number with recent sales of renovated homes near the address.

Do I need to register a Louisville rental I just bought?

Yes. Louisville Metro requires rental properties to be registered with Codes and Regulations, and a registration does not transfer when a property is sold. The city can fine up to $100 a day per unit for failing to register.
