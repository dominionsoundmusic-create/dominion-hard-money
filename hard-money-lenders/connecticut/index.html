Hard Money Lenders in Connecticut

In Connecticut, the town line can move a tax bill sevenfold on the same house.

Get your deal reviewedRefinance into a DSCR loan

The same $400,000 house can carry a tax bill near $19,000 in Hartford and under $3,500 in Greenwich. Here is how that works, what the conveyance tax, strict foreclosure and older housing stock do to a deal, and how our lending partners size the loan.

The town line is worth more than the property

Connecticut assesses every property at 70 percent of fair market value, then each municipality applies its own mill rate: one mill being one dollar of tax per $1,000 of assessed value. Towns set those rates independently through their own budgets, and nothing pulls them toward each other. Towns must also revalue at least every five years, so a revaluation can reset every assessment in town at once.

The result is a spread that has to be seen in dollars. Take the same $400,000 house, assessed at $280,000. Hartford's real property rate has been close to 69 mills, which puts the bill near $19,000 a year. Greenwich's rate has been around 10 to 11 mills, which puts the same house under $3,500. That is close to a factor of seven, same building, same state. And the cities with the best rent-to-price ratios, such as Hartford, Waterbury and Bridgeport, tend to carry the heaviest rates.

Why: those cities hold large amounts of tax-exempt property. Hospitals, universities and state buildings sit off the grand list while still needing services, so the burden falls on what remains taxable, which is largely rental property and small business.

Connecticut also has no general homestead exemption, so unlike states such as Alabama or Florida, a rental is not taxed on a different basis from the owner's home next door. The difference is entirely about the town. Two details catch out-of-state buyers. Special service districts can stack on top of the town rate and rarely appear in statewide tables. And a falling mill rate after a revaluation is not a tax cut: when assessed values rise, the rate falls to collect roughly the same levy against a bigger base, so read the new assessment and the new rate together.

The practical instruction: pull the current mill rate for the exact address from the town assessor or the state Office of Policy and Management before you write the offer. Not the county rate, not a statewide average, and not a third-party calculator that may be a year or more behind.

The conveyance tax hits the exit

Connecticut's real estate conveyance tax is normally paid by the seller, so for a flipper it lands on the sale, not the purchase. The state takes 0.75 percent of the first $800,000 of a residential sale, with higher rates on the portion above that. The municipality adds 0.25 percent in most towns, and a group of targeted investment communities, including Hartford, Bridgeport, New Haven, Waterbury and New Britain, can charge more. So in exactly the cities where flips pencil, budget more than 1 percent of your sale price. On a $400,000 exit at 1.25 percent that is $5,000, and it belongs in the model before you offer.

It is also why the refinance exit earns its keep here. Refinancing a finished project into a longer-term rental loan involves no sale, so there is no conveyance tax.

Strict foreclosure: how Connecticut takes back a house

Connecticut forecloses through the courts, and it is one of the few states that commonly uses strict foreclosure. Instead of an auction, the court sets "law days." The owner, then each junior lienholder in turn, has until its law day to pay off the debt and redeem. If nobody does, title passes to the foreclosing lender without a sale. The court can order a foreclosure by sale instead, usually when there is meaningful equity, and owner-occupants can ask for mediation, which adds time.

For an investor this means two things. There are fewer courthouse auctions than in sale states, so many distressed Connecticut deals are bought from the lender after it takes title, or from the owner before the law day. And because the process runs through the court, it is slow: budget for a long timeline if you are buying a note or waiting on a foreclosure, and have a title company confirm that every law day has passed before you close.

The older-housing checks: foundations and lead

Crumbling foundations. In parts of north-central and eastern Connecticut, concrete containing the mineral pyrrhotite has caused basement walls to crack and fail, often years after the house was built. The problem centers on homes from roughly the 1980s through the early 2000s in towns around Tolland and Windham counties and the eastern side of Hartford County. Replacing a foundation is a project that can exceed the value of a flip's margin, and standard homeowners policies generally do not cover it. On any house in the affected area, get a core test or a qualified inspection before you commit, and expect a buyer's inspector to ask.

Lead paint. Much of the yield stock in Hartford, New Haven, Waterbury and Bridgeport is older multifamily, built long before lead paint was banned in 1978. Federal disclosure rules apply on every sale and lease, and Connecticut requires abatement when a child with an elevated blood lead level is found in a unit. Price lead-safe work into any renovation of a pre-1978 rental.

Where the deals are

Hartford: the yield city, if you can carry the tax

The stock is turn-of-the-century multifamily, converted brick mills and New England triple-deckers, and it varies sharply by neighborhood. The South End is the cash-flow play: sturdy two-to-four-unit buildings at the lower end of the city's prices. The West End is the opposite trade: Victorian and Queen Anne streetscapes near Elizabeth Park, higher entry prices and lower yields, but steady tenants. Parkville sits between them, near the CTfastrak line. On every Hartford deal, the tax line is the first number to check.

New Britain: two-families near Hartford

About ten miles from Hartford, with 1920s two-families that renovate cleanly and rental demand from Central Connecticut State University and Hartford commuters, which gives a real fallback if a flip stalls. The buyer pool is price sensitive, so overshoot the finish level and the house sits.

Waterbury: cheapest entry, slower sales

Among the lowest entry prices of the larger Connecticut cities, offset by a high mill rate and a market that can move slowly. Budget carry for a longer hold than the spreadsheet assumes.

Bridgeport and the coast

Bridgeport sits in the Stamford to New Haven corridor, one of the most competitive rental markets in the Northeast, with rents above the inland cities. In Fairfield and New Haven counties prices are high relative to rent, so a leveraged rental there is usually an appreciation or value-add thesis rather than day-one cash flow. Be honest about which one you are buying.

Connecticut landlord rules that affect a rental's numbers

Security deposits are capped at two months' rent, or one month for a tenant 62 or older, and held in an escrow account with interest paid to the tenant. The landlord has 21 days after the tenant moves out to return the deposit or send an itemized list of deductions.

Late rent. Connecticut gives tenants a grace period before rent counts as late, and caps late fees. An eviction (summary process) starts with a notice to quit and runs through housing court, so budget for vacancy time on a nonpaying unit.

A DSCR loan is sized on the rent the property can carry, with the real Connecticut tax bill inside the payment. In a high-mill city that line can decide whether the ratio clears.

What gets a Connecticut deal declined

A tax figure taken from the county or a state average. The most expensive Connecticut mistake. The town sets the rate, and the spread approaches sevenfold.

A model that reads a mill rate cut as a tax cut. Check the new assessment alongside the new rate in any town that recently revalued.

An exit budgeted at one percent. In the targeted investment communities the conveyance tax can run higher.

An untested foundation in the pyrrhotite area. It can turn a flip into a loss and a buyer's inspector will find it.

Questions investors ask

Can I get a hard money loan anywhere in Connecticut?

Our lending partners review deals across the state: Hartford, New Haven and Fairfield counties, plus Litchfield, Middlesex, New London, Tolland and Windham. Each deal is underwritten on its own value, cost and your experience, and the lender decides.

How do I find the mill rate for a Connecticut property?

Get the current rate from the town assessor or the state Office of Policy and Management's town-by-town list, and check whether the town has revalued recently. Then apply it to 70 percent of the value you expect.

Who pays the conveyance tax when I sell a flip in Connecticut?

Normally the seller. The state portion is 0.75 percent of the first $800,000 on a residential sale, and the town adds 0.25 percent in most places and can charge more in targeted investment communities such as Hartford and Bridgeport.

Are there foreclosure auctions in Connecticut?

Fewer than in most states. Connecticut often uses strict foreclosure, where title passes to the lender after the law days run out, with no auction. Foreclosure by sale is ordered in some cases, usually when there is equity.

Should I worry about crumbling foundations on a Connecticut flip?

In north-central and eastern Connecticut, yes. Pyrrhotite in concrete has damaged foundations in many homes built from roughly the 1980s to the early 2000s. Get the foundation tested before you buy, because a replacement can erase the profit.

Should I flip or refinance and hold in Connecticut?

Flip where the after-repair value is well supported and homes are selling quickly. Refinance into a DSCR loan where resale softens and rent is strong, which also avoids the conveyance tax because there is no sale.
